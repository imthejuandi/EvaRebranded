## Correct the proposed premise

Do not say “lab reference ranges are just an average of the population and the population is not healthy on average.” A reference interval is not an arithmetic average. Many population-based intervals describe the distribution in selected, apparently healthy reference individuals, often the central 95%; selection criteria, method, population and partitioning matter. Clinical decision limits are a different concept and may come from clinical outcomes, diagnostic performance or expert guidelines. Reports may display such limits alongside, or under terminology similar to, reference ranges.

The 95% convention belongs in this evidence explanation, not as a new statistic in the proposed landing copy. It does not establish how the selected Valley Hospital <3 mg/L limit was derived. That catalog publishes the limit but does not document its derivation. Do not infer that this particular bar is an average-population interval, or that it was developed from an unhealthy population.

## Concise proposed paragraph

**EN:** A lab’s reference range helps interpret a result, but it may differ from levels linked to lower long-term risk. EVA connects that result with your health history and context, helping you understand the fuller picture.

**ES:** El rango de referencia de un laboratorio ayuda a interpretar un resultado, pero puede diferir de los niveles asociados a un menor riesgo a largo plazo. EVA conecta ese resultado con tu historial de salud y tu contexto para ayudarte a entender el conjunto.

This medically approved variant uses “context” so it also fits someone without previous readings. The copy specialist’s alternative “your health history and previous readings” / “tu historial de salud y tus mediciones anteriores” is also medically acceptable when previous readings are available; it is a product-scope wording choice, not a clinical disagreement. “May differ” is deliberate. “Linked to” describes an association, not guaranteed benefit from lowering the biomarker.

## Defensible patient benefit

The supported benefit is **better understanding of a result in context**: a person can see why being inside one lab’s reference does not, by itself, settle questions about relative cardiovascular risk, other risk factors or the meaning of their health history. This can inform a more focused discussion, without promising that the service predicts disease, prevents an event or improves lifespan.

Do not claim EVA’s interpretation has demonstrated improved patient outcomes, longer life, earlier diagnosis or quantified risk reduction on the basis of these sources. Do not portray established laboratory practice as careless or based on an unhealthy population. The selected reference and the risk category have different uses; one does not invalidate the other.

It describes health-associated reference values, reference-subject selection, analytical/preanalytical considerations and transfer/verification of intervals. The public abstract/scope was reviewed; no claim of reviewing the paid full standard.
   https://clsi.org/shop/standards/ep28/

2. **CLSI Harmonized Terminology Database**, biological reference interval entry incorporating ISO/IFCC terminology. Search-indexed official text identifies a specified interval of a reference-population distribution, commonly central 95%, with exceptions and one-sided limits. Direct page open timed out, so use is bounded to the indexed official definition, corroborated by the committee sources.
   https://htd.clsi.org/listterms.asp?button=Submit

3. **Ozarda Y et al., on behalf of the IFCC Committee on Reference Intervals and Decision Limits (2018)**. Distinguishing reference intervals and clinical decision limits. Official committee review, Crit Rev Clin Lab Sci 55(6):420–431. PubMed/journal abstract reviewed: intervals describe a healthy reference distribution; decision limits relate to particular clinical outcomes or diagnostic decisions. This is authoritative committee guidance, not an intervention trial demonstrating benefit from EVA.

## Final mechanism-inclusive refinement — supersedes the earlier preferred paragraph

Root requested that the reference-population explanation appear visibly, rather than solely in the proposal rationale. **Medical PASS** on the copy specialist’s exact current English:

> Many lab reference ranges describe typical results in selected groups of apparently healthy people. They don’t always identify levels linked to lower long-term risk. EVA explains the difference in plain language and puts your results in context with your health history.

The root wording “Many lab reference ranges describe what’s typical in a selected group of healthy people. They don’t always show which levels are linked to lower long-term risk. EVA explains the difference in plain language, putting your results in context with your health history” also passes with the recommended addition **apparently healthy**. “Healthy reference population” is common in the literature, but “apparently healthy” more accurately avoids claiming that screened reference individuals have perfect or fully known health.

“Many” correctly scopes the population-based statement; “don’t always” avoids asserting that laboratory reference values can never incorporate risk thresholds. “Linked” stays associative. The benefit remains explanation and context, with no disease, optimum, treatment or outcome promise. No new statistic or recommendation is introduced.

Chart adjacency is **not a blocker**: this is a qualified general statement and does not explicitly assert that The Valley Hospital’s <3 mg/L limit was derived from population percentiles. The named laboratory/source detail should remain, and the owner-facing rationale should candidly state that its catalog does not document derivation. Do not rewrite the general statement as “This lab range is the average of healthy people.”
